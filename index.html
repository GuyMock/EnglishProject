English Portfolio

Anthony Mauclair - IB English Language and Literature SL

IO

Global issue: public narratives legitimise violence as moral duty.
Field of inquiry: power, politics and justice.

Body of work: three recruitment posters by the Parliamentary Recruiting Committee (1914-1916). The PRC produced around 200 designs. No conscription until January 1916, so every man addressed could legally say no. The whole campaign ran on persuasion, mostly shame.

Literary texts: three Larkin poems, from The Whitsun Weddings and High Windows. The posters work before the harm, they make violence look like duty so it happens. Larkin works after, on the words that make what already happened bearable.

Posters

1. Your King and Country Need You

  • Direct address. "Your" makes the war a private relationship, so not enlisting stops being a decision and starts being disloyalty.
  • Soldier dead centre, life-size, gaze meets the viewer. One man being addressed, not a crowd.
  • Clean uniform, calm face. No blood, no mud, no enemy anywhere. The absence is the argument, it persuades by removing the war.
  • "Honour and glory" - abstract nouns replace physical suffering. Defence of national character, not killing.
  • "A wee scrap o' paper is Britain's bond" - a treaty becomes a test of personal integrity.

2. Britain Needs You at Once

  • St George and the dragon. Modern industrial war rewritten as medieval heroism.
  • The enemy is a monster, not a person, so fighting stops feeling like harming people.
  • White horse against dark dragon, Britain as innocence in a binary.
  • "At once" removes deliberation. You are not asked to consider the war, only to arrive at it.
  • First poster removes the war, this one removes the enemy. St George also brings religious authority in, which comes back in the third poster.

3. It Is Far Better to Face the Bullets Than to Be Killed at Home by a Bomb

  • 1915, voluntary enlistment falling. Honour dropped for fear.
  • Dark palette, Zeppelin over London. The home front becomes a target (raids had started that year).
  • False dilemma: face bullets abroad or wait to be killed at home. Staying is never allowed to look safe.
  • A man who stays looks like he is hiding behind his family, not protecting it.
  • The move from poster 1 to this one shows the campaign losing confidence in its own moral case. Honour worked in 1914, by 1915 it needed fear underneath.

Larkin

1. Essential Beauty

  • Adverts, not war, but the same mechanism.
  • "Block the ends of streets" - the hoardings obstruct what is behind them, they do not decorate.
  • "Screen graves with custard, cover slums with praise" - hiding, not fixing. Nothing improves, it just stops being visible.
  • "How life should be" - the world being sold never existed.
  • Ends with the dying smoker seeing the woman from the cigarette advert. The image that harmed him is his last comfort. Same direction as the posters: honour laid over the war the way custard is laid over graves.

2. Homage to a Government

  • 1968 withdrawal from east of Suez.
  • "And it is all right" repeated. It sounds reasonable, the reasonableness is what unsettles.
  • Same rhyme words in every stanza, the language circles without developing. That is how official justification behaves.
  • "For lack of money" strips patriotism out. Duty becomes accounting.
  • Contradicts the posters: governments build narratives of duty when they need them and drop them when they don't.

3. The Explosion

  • "Fathers, brothers, nicknames, laughter" - the miners get individuality before public memory turns them into symbols.
  • After the disaster: "larger than in life", "gold as on a coin". Steady hymn-like rhythm.
  • Last line stands alone, one of the dead showing the eggs unbroken. A gesture aimed at the living.
  • Complicates the global issue. This is idealisation after death, it comforts rather than recruits, and Larkin does not mock it.
  • Some narratives grow out of grief. They turn dangerous when suffering is made so noble that criticising it feels cruel, which is also how the posters protect themselves.

Paper 1

Guided analysis of a non-literary text. The job is not describing everything, it is explaining how selected choices build the message.

Intro: text type, content in one line, who made it, context, tone, purpose, audience, message.

Body paragraphs, PEEL:

  • Point - a claim about a feature
  • Example - exactly what is visible or quoted
  • Explanation - method, effect, how it positions the audience
  • Link - back to purpose or message

Visual texts: start with whatever the eye lands on first. Then composition, colour and contrast, gaze, text and typography. Everything in the frame is deliberate.

Start with what is explicitly visible, then what it implies, then the wider message. No guessing motives or private thoughts. Short quotes, properly analysed. If time is nearly up, close the paragraph, never start a new one.

Glossary

  • Active voice - the subject does the action: "the crowd cheered".
  • Adjective - a describing word attached to a noun.
  • Adverb - modifies a verb or adjective: how, when, how often.
  • Alliteration - repeated consonant sounds at the starts of nearby words.
  • Allusion - an indirect reference to another text, event or cultural idea.
  • Ambiguity - wording that genuinely supports more than one reading.
  • Analysis - explaining how a choice creates meaning, not just naming it.
  • Anaphora - starting successive lines or clauses with the same words.
  • Angle - where the camera or artist places the viewer relative to the subject.
  • Antithesis - two opposed ideas set against each other in balanced phrasing.
  • Aside - a line spoken to the audience that other characters cannot hear.
  • Assonance - repeated vowel sounds inside nearby words.
  • Audience - who the text is actually built for.
  • Authorial intent - what the writer seems to want the audience to think or feel.
  • Background - whatever sits behind the main subject.
  • Blank verse - unrhymed lines in iambic pentameter.
  • Bleed - when an image runs off the edge of the frame with no border.
  • Body of work - several related non-literary texts by the same creator.
  • Brand identity - the consistent personality a brand performs across its output.
  • Caesura - a pause in the middle of a line, usually from punctuation.
  • Call to action - the line telling the audience exactly what to do next.
  • Caption - the written line under or inside an image.
  • Caricature - exaggerating someone's features to make a point about them.
  • Claim - an arguable point that evidence can support.
  • Clause - a unit of language with its own subject and verb.
  • Cliche - a phrase so overused it has stopped meaning anything.
  • Colloquial language - casual, everyday speech showing up in print.
  • Colon - introduces a list, an explanation or an emphasis.
  • Colour palette - the overall range of colours an image commits to.
  • Comma - the light pause.
  • Comparative thesis - one argument covering how both texts treat the same idea.
  • Composition - how everything in the frame is arranged.
  • Connotation - what a word suggests beyond its literal meaning.
  • Context - the circumstances around a text: social, historical, cultural.
  • Contraction - shortened forms like can't and I'm.
  • Contrast - a strong difference the text forces you to notice.
  • Critique - when a text challenges an idea or system instead of just showing it.
  • Crop - what the frame deliberately cuts out.
  • Dash - punctuation of interruption or a sudden change of direction.
  • Declarative sentence - a plain statement of fact or belief.
  • Demotic language - ordinary, street-level speech.
  • Denotation - the flat dictionary meaning of a word.
  • Depth of field - how much of the image stays in focus front to back.
  • Dialogue - characters talking to each other.
  • Diction - the writer's word choices.
  • Direct gaze - the subject looks straight out at the viewer.
  • Dramatic irony - the audience knows something a character doesn't.
  • Ellipsis - three dots marking something left unsaid.
  • Emanata - cartoon marks for motion or feeling, sweat drops, radiating lines.
  • Emotional blackmail - persuasion that works through guilt or shame.
  • Emotive language - words picked to make you feel before you think.
  • End-stopped line - a poetic line that closes with punctuation.
  • Endorsement - a respected figure lending authority to the message.
  • Enjambment - the sentence spills past the end of the line.
  • Euphemism - a soft phrase laid over a hard truth.
  • Evaluation - judging how well a choice works, not just describing it.
  • Evidence - the actual words or details a point is built on.
  • Exclamation mark - emphasis, force, volume.
  • Expletive - swearing, or a sudden forceful outburst.
  • Field of inquiry - the broad territory a global issue sits inside.
  • Figurative language - anything not meant literally.
  • First person - I, me, my.
  • Foreground - the front of the image, nearest the viewer.
  • Foreshadowing - an early hint of what is coming later.
  • Framing - where the borders fall and what they trap inside.
  • Free verse - poetry with no fixed meter or rhyme scheme.
  • Full stop - the hard stop.
  • Global issue - an issue with real weight across countries and cultures.
  • Graphic weight - how heavily an element pulls the eye.
  • Grid layout - content locked into strict rows and columns.
  • Gutter - the gap between comic panels.
  • Hook - the opening line that earns the audience's attention.
  • Hyperbole - deliberate exaggeration.
  • Iambic pentameter - ten syllables, unstressed then stressed, five per line.
  • Iconography - symbols a culture reads instantly.
  • Idiom - a fixed phrase that doesn't mean its literal words.
  • Imagery - language that makes you see, hear or feel the thing.
  • Imperative verb - a command: go, stop, enlist.
  • Inference - a conclusion the evidence supports without stating it outright.
  • Internal rhyme - rhyme inside the line rather than at its end.
  • Interpretation - my reading of what a detail means.
  • Interrogative sentence - a sentence in the form of a question.
  • Irony - the gap between what is said or shown and what is meant.
  • Juxtaposition - two contrasting things placed right next to each other.
  • Label - words inside a cartoon naming what things stand for.
  • Layout - where text and images sit on the page.
  • Lexical field - a cluster of words drawn from the same topic.
  • Line of argument - the through-line of an essay, each point building on the last.
  • Logo - the brand's visual signature.
  • Message - the idea the text wants to leave you holding.
  • Metaphor - saying one thing is another thing.
  • Meter - the rhythm pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables.
  • Minor sentence - a fragment used deliberately.
  • Modal verb - might, must, should, could.
  • Monologue - one character speaking at length.
  • Mood - what the audience is made to feel.
  • Motif - an image or idea that keeps returning through the text.
  • Narrator - the voice telling the story, not always the author.
  • Negative space - the emptiness around the subject.
  • Noun phrase - a noun plus the words modifying it.
  • Onomatopoeia - words that sound like the thing they mean.
  • Oxymoron - two contradictory words jammed together.
  • Panel - one frame of a comic sequence.
  • Paradox - a contradiction that turns out to hold a truth.
  • Parallelism - repeating the same grammatical shape across phrases.
  • Parentheses - brackets tucking an extra thought into a sentence.
  • Passive voice - the action happens to the subject, the doer can vanish.
  • Peer pressure appeal - everyone else is doing it, so you should too.
  • Persona - the constructed speaker, a mask the writer performs through.
  • Personification - giving human qualities to something non-human.
  • Perspective (argument) - the position a text views an issue from.
  • Perspective (visual) - the viewpoint built into the image itself.
  • Pronoun - I, we, you, they. "We" recruits the reader, "they" builds the wall.
  • Proportion - the relative sizes of things inside the image.
  • Pun - wordplay built on a double meaning or a sound.
  • Purpose - why the text exists at all.
  • Question mark - marks a question, real or rhetorical.
  • Reader positioning - how the text moves you into a particular viewpoint.
  • Refrain - the line that keeps coming back, usually in songs.
  • Register - how formal or informal the language is.
  • Repetition - saying it again, on purpose.
  • Rhetorical question - a question that isn't really asking.
  • Rule of thirds - subjects sit on the grid lines, not dead centre.
  • Salience - whatever your eye hits first.
  • Scale - size relative to the frame or to other elements.
  • Second person - you, your. The text reaching out of the page.
  • Semantic field - a set of words connected by shared meaning.
  • Semicolon - links two clauses that belong together.
  • Setting - the place or environment a scene happens in.
  • Shot distance - how close the subject appears in the frame.
  • Significance - why the detail matters to the whole text.
  • Simile - a comparison using like or as.
  • Slang - in-group informal language.
  • Slogan - the short line engineered to be remembered.
  • Soliloquy - a character alone on stage, thinking out loud.
  • Speaker - the voice in a poem or lyric, not the poet.
  • Speech bubble - the container for dialogue in a cartoon.
  • Stanza - a poem's version of a paragraph.
  • Structure - the organising shape of the whole text.
  • Symbolism - a concrete thing standing in for an abstract one.
  • Synthesis - pulling separate threads together into one larger claim.
  • Syntax - how the sentence itself is built.
  • Target audience - who the text is engineered to reach. Be specific.
  • Theme - the underlying concern the text keeps circling.
  • Thesis - the essay's central argument in one or two sentences.
  • Third person - he, she, they.
  • Tone - the writer's attitude toward the subject.
  • Topic sentence - the first line of a paragraph, carrying its claim.
  • Transnational - crosses borders and cultures.
  • Tricolon - three parallel items in a row.
  • Typography - the design of the text itself: font, size, weight, spacing.
  • Understatement - playing something down on purpose.
  • Verb - the action word of the sentence.
  • Visual hierarchy - the order the design makes you read things in.
  • Visual pun - a joke made with images instead of words.
  • Volta - the turn, where a poem changes direction.
  • White space - the deliberate emptiness left in a design.
  • Zoom in - close analysis of one small choice.
  • Zoom out - connecting that choice back to the whole text or the global issue.
  • Zoomorphism - describing a person through animal traits.
